Two train hours away. The river runs through it visibly — not channeled underground, actually present, with banks you can walk on. The older part of the city grew around the river in the way older cities grew around water, and the streets follow its logic rather than any grid.

The market is smaller than what you're used to if you come from the city, which means you see the same vendors two weeks in a row and they remember you. The bread comes from two bakers, both on the main square, and the people here have strong opinions about which is better. The debate has been running for longer than anyone currently alive.

The buildings are lower. Four stories is tall here; most things are two or three. This changes the light — more of it, more directly, the streets not shadowed by their buildings in the way city streets shadow themselves. In summer the squares are genuinely bright at noon, which is uncomfortable in a way you get used to.

People here know each other in the way you know people when the city is small enough to maintain. Not everyone — it's still a city, not a village — but the overlap is denser. You recognize faces from the tram that you also recognize from the market that you also recognize from the pool. This can be a comfort or an annoyance depending on your disposition.

They come here for the weekend sometimes — the river, the bread debate, the lower ceilings, the feeling of being in a place that runs at a slightly different speed. It's not slower, exactly. It's just calibrated to a different scale.

By Sunday afternoon they're ready to go back. That seems right. You don't stay in someone else's city. You visit it and then you go home to yours.
