The workshop was on the ground floor, street-facing, and smelled like flux. There was a handwritten sign on the door: *ring and wait, in the middle of something.*

She rang. She waited.

Daphne came out wiping her hands on a cloth. "The OX-25B? Yeah, come in."

It was on the bench, screen glowing green, a waveform sitting steady on it. Not plugged into anything interesting — just a test signal, Daphne checking something — but it was running. The same green. The same particular shade of it.

"Caps had gone," Daphne said, setting down the cloth. "Electrolytics in the HV section — pretty common for that era. One coupling cap had a hairline crack in it, probably from being dropped at some point. Four cold joints I reflowed while I was in there. Calibration's good. I ran it for a few hours before I called you."

She handed over the ticket with the notes on it. Neat handwriting. *Should be good for another few decades.*

She said nothing for a moment. The waveform sat there, steady.

Her father had used this to trace a fault in a conveyor controller in 1994 and come home smelling like machine oil, and she'd been seven and asked what the squiggly line meant, and he'd said *that's what electricity looks like when it's being honest.*

She didn't say any of that. Daphne was already back at something on the other bench.

"What do I owe you?"

Daphne told her. It was reasonable.

She paid, wrapped the scope in the cloth she'd brought, and carried it down the stairs.
