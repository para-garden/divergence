By August the city has adapted. Not solved — it's still hot, the flats on the south side are too warm in the afternoon, the tram gets close and airless by noon — but adapted, in the way that people adapt to things that come every year.

The market starts earlier. Six-thirty, some of the vendors, to sell before the heat takes the produce. The stone bench in the courtyard holds the coolness of night longer than the air does; people use this. The pool is full by nine.

People eat outside in the evening because outside at nine is better than inside. The tables appear in the courtyard, on balconies, on the pieces of pavement that get shade by six. Food is simpler in August — less oven, more things that are good cold or at room temperature. The produce is at its peak. The tomatoes from the roof are extraordinary this year, Kofi says, which means he's pleased, which he doesn't say often.

The city sounds different in August. More windows open means more of everyone's lives leak into everyone else's — music from three directions, conversations in the courtyard that carry further than expected in the still air, the cat that wanders more at night because the nights are warm.

Some people leave for a while. Others stay and find the city with fewer people in it briefly has a quality — more space, the streets slightly more yours, the bakery with more tables available on a Saturday. Marta takes a week in August. She's done this for ten years. The bakery has a sign during that week. It's still there when she comes back.

By September you want it to end and by October you've already forgotten that the heat was something you wanted to end.
