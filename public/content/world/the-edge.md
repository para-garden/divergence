You don't cross a line. The city just becomes less itself gradually and then you're somewhere that isn't it.

The last tram stop before the farm roads is still a city stop — there's a bench, a shelter, a schedule. But the smell is different. There's more sky. The sounds change: less street, more wind, occasionally something that might be an animal. You're still fifteen minutes from the center and it already feels like you've arrived at a different register.

The fields here are used. Not decorative — used, in the practical sense of things being grown and harvested and made into food that ends up in the market. The tractors are real. There's mud when it rains. The farm roads have names that describe where they go, not who used to own them.

Some people come out here just to be here. Mostly on weekends. They walk the road along the field edge or sit somewhere that has the horizon in it and let the city recede. There's no special destination. The point is the distance.

The fox has a territory that covers part of this. The plum trees at the edge of the farm are in it. So are the back gardens of the last buildings before the density thins. She moves between registers without noticing — city fox, field fox, edge fox. The territory is what it is.

At the last tram stop someone chalked something on the shelter wall last spring that mostly washed off in the rain. You can still read part of it: a date, and then *the field was this green.* Apparently someone wanted that noted.
