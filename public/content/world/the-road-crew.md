The section near the canal has been waiting for repair since the frost heaved it in March. Four people, a van with the equipment, the week finally available in May.

They're not strangers to each other — this crew has been working together for two years, enough time to have found the rhythm of it. Who does what. How fast they move. When to stop for coffee and when to push through.

The work is what it is: cut the damaged section, prepare the base, relay the surface, let it cure. Nothing unexpected in this section. A job that goes the way it's supposed to go is not uninteresting to the people doing it — good work has its own quality, the feel of something being right.

They work and talk and work. Mostly work. The conversation is the kind you have alongside something physical — incomplete sentences, references to things only the four of them know about, occasional silence that isn't uncomfortable. One of them has a kid who started walking last week. He mentioned this on Monday and they talked about it for the appropriate amount of time and that was that.

By Thursday the section is done. They stand back. It's just a road, finished correctly, which means you won't know it's been repaired in six months. The canal path runs alongside it and the paving stones are level and the tram won't catch on anything there anymore. Nobody will think about this road.

That's what it's supposed to be. That's what they built.
