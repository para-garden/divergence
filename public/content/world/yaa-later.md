She ended up learning the CAD. Not instead of fabrication — in addition. Ama was right that you own the thing end to end if you can do both, but what Yaa found was that it made her better at fabrication too: she can see what she's making before she makes it and know where it will fail. Kwame said *told you* about the hands but she thinks he also didn't fully expect this. He adapts his opinion carefully when someone proves it more right than he meant.

There's a kid now — Tomasz, seventeen, just started — who has the thing Yaa used to have, the spatial sense, the patience with the machine, the wanting-to-know. She doesn't mentor him in any formal way. She shows him things when he's watching. She told him what she told herself: if a piece comes off the bed and feels wrong in your hand, it is wrong. Trust that before you trust the file.

The fans are still rattly. She put in a request three months ago. Nothing's happened. The bee-cup sound remains.

She went home to visit last month — her mother's kitchen, groundnut cake, her cousin Ama who was right about everything and knows it. She came back knowing what she'd built here was actually what she wanted, which is the thing visiting home does when you've built the right thing.
