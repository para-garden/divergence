The market at Tamale opens before the light does. Abena has her groundnut cakes out by five, wrapped in newspaper because she likes the smell and the ink never comes off on your hands anymore — they changed the formula sometime in the twenties, she doesn't know when. The plastic bags she used to use, then didn't, then used again — she stopped keeping track of what was good and what wasn't, just uses what's there.

By six the light is coming and the street is loud with yelling and generators and someone's phone playing something with a lot of bass. Her daughter comes by on the way to the fabrication workshop, steals a cake without paying, gets told off, doesn't care. She's eighteen. She'll come back for lunch.

A dog sleeps under the table. Nobody owns it and nobody doesn't own it. It knows where the food is. It knows Abena's voice.

Two men argue about a wall — someone painted something on a compound wall on the east side, very beautiful, also very large, also not their wall. The owner of the wall doesn't mind. His wife minds. The argument isn't about that though, it's about whether you need to ask first or whether asking first is missing the point of what makes a thing like that good. They're both right. They argue for another twenty minutes and part as friends, no resolution, not embarrassed by this.

The heat comes up fast. By eight it's real. Abena sells out by ten-thirty.
