Five years. That's enough that the neighborhood knows her in the way it knows people — the bakery has her order ready, the market vendors nod, the tram driver who does the morning shift recognizes her face the way you recognize faces without quite knowing why.

She stopped noticing when this happened. At some point the city went from a place she was learning to a place she knew. She can't locate the moment. It was gradual and then it was just done.

The flat is hers. Not owned — she rents it, the building has a landlord somewhere — but hers in the sense of shaped by her presence, the way things accumulate over five years. The plant she bought the second month, still there, taller. The wall she repainted once, a color she likes, that now she doesn't notice because it's just the color of the wall. The shelf she built badly and had to redo and built right the second time and still slightly can't look at without seeing the first version.

She knows where the light lands in the afternoon. She knows which neighbors she'll see at the recycling bins. She knows the sound the building makes in wind, which she can distinguish from the sound it makes in hard rain, which is different from the sound it makes when someone on the fifth floor is moving furniture.

None of this is significant. It's just the material of a life lived in a place. The place holds the material and gives it back to you as context. You go somewhere else and you don't have this and you have to build it again.

This is where she lives. It's where she lives. That's the whole statement.
