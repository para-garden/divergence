Marta is in at four-thirty. The dough that started the night before has done what it needed to do. She shapes the loaves in the dark — not completely, the counter light is on — and puts them to proof.

The croissant lamination is the early work. Cold butter, cold dough, cold hands preferred. She's been doing this for eleven years and she still respects the temperature. Some mornings she gets it perfect and some mornings it's adequate and a few times something has gone wrong in a way that means a different product than planned. She writes nothing down about this. She knows.

The oven is on by five. The building warms up.

First customers at six-thirty are the ones who need to be somewhere. They know what they want. She knows what they want. The transaction is quick and both parties find this satisfying. A man who works at the fabrication workshop comes in early every day and asks for the same thing and she has it ready by the time he's put his bag down. They have never discussed this arrangement. It's just how it goes.

The middle hour is the lingerers — people who have time, who want to stand for a minute. The smell in the shop is at its peak. She's been inside it so long she doesn't smell it anymore except occasionally when a cold morning and the right bread overlap in a specific way and it gets through.

She closes at two. The bread that's left she prices down at one. By two, usually, there isn't much left. She cleans. She goes. The oven holds its heat for a while after.

She likes the early shift. You get the city before it's decided anything. Later in the day it's already somebody else's.
