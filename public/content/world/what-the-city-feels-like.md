The paving stones are cold underfoot through shoes in January. Not through shoes — you feel it, the cold coming up. In July the same stones hold the sun's heat until eleven at night and you can feel it through sandals.

The handrail on the canal bridge is smooth from hands. Not polished — just the specific smoothness of something that's been touched by thousands of hands at the same point, which is how rails get smooth. The rivets are painted over, then painted over again; you can feel the texture of the layers if you stop to look.

The air in the market at six in the morning in April: cold still, slightly damp, the first smell of bread from the bakery van that comes at five-thirty, and underneath it the smell of the morning, which is nothing specific and is also specific to mornings.

Rain on the street has a sound that changes with the surface. Different from rain on the canal. Different from rain on the courtyard's stone, which is different from rain on the raised beds with their soil. You can tell from inside your flat, if you've been here long enough, which kind of rain is landing on what.

The tram in motion: the particular sway of it, the sound the wheels make on the tracks in the section near the canal where the tracks are older and not quite flat anymore. The smell of the tram in August, which is unavoidable, and the specific relief of the tram in January when you've been standing outside.

The city is not an idea. It's a set of surfaces and sounds and smells that have been accumulating for a long time. You live in it with your body. Your body knows it before your mind catches up.
