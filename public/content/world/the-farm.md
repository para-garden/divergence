The farm is fifteen minutes by bike from the center of the city. Not "the outskirts." Just the city, then a field. Nobody designed the boundary. It's where the buildings stopped being dense enough to be a city and started being something else.

In July there are tomatoes in long rows that you could see from the road if you looked. In October there are the root vegetables and the squash in varieties that don't have well-known names, just names, and the farm stand operates three days a week with whatever that week produced. Seasonal means actually seasonal — if you want tomatoes in February you don't get them from here.

Some of the market vendors are from here. Some are from farms further out. The man who sells the apple varieties that bruise easily — you can ask him which field, he'll tell you. This information is not particularly useful to most people but it's available.

Kids from the city come sometimes. School groups, learning-groups, whoever. They come to see where things come from, and then they come back because the farm is also just a place that's there, fifteen minutes out, and sometimes you want to go somewhere with more horizon than your street gives you.

The plums from the market — the good ones, September, the kind that are actually ripe — come from here. The fox that moves through the city at night goes through this field too. There are plum trees at the edge, where the farm meets a path, and in September there are windfalls under them that the fox finds before the morning.

The boundary between city and farm is a road and then it isn't anymore.
