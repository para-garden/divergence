Léa is nine. She argues about things now — not about being told to do something, but about whether the thing is correct. She'll say *but why is that how it works* and mean it as a real question. Her father finds this exhausting in the specific way of being proud of something that is also a lot.

What he thinks about: whether she has enough people she's comfortable with, because she's on the quieter end and he worries sometimes that quiet gets mistaken for fine. Whether the thing she's interested in this month — right now it's water, specifically where water goes when it goes somewhere — is getting enough room. Whether the people around her see the thing he sees when she's working something out.

He doesn't think about: saving for her future in any specific way, because the future she's going into isn't one where the floor is missing and she'll need to stockpile before she's ten. He thinks about whether she's happy today. Yesterday she was. Tomorrow seems likely.

He worries about the concrete things. Whether she's eating enough. The friendship situation, which is good but he keeps an eye on. The sleep, which fluctuates. The water obsession, which is probably fine, probably the beginning of something, who knows.

He doesn't know what she'll do when she's older and doesn't spend much time trying to figure it out. She'll do something. She'll figure it out. She's nine and she's already figuring out where water goes, which is more initiative than he had at nine.

Yesterday she asked him how rivers decide which way to flow. He said he wasn't totally sure and they should look it up. They looked it up. She had follow-up questions that lasted until dinner.

He thinks about that on the tram this morning. Just: that happened.
