The flat on the third floor has a ceiling six meters high, which sounds luxurious and is mostly impractical — the space at the top is cold in winter and the light from the industrial windows comes in high and horizontal. The person who lives there has figured out the winter by hanging things. Textiles, not for decoration, for insulation; it works. The windows she's stopped trying to fix. The light is what it is and the light is interesting.

She moved in four years ago when the third floor was the last one finished. The building had been converting for years — slowly, as people came and the builders did the next section. She moved into something that smelled of fresh plaster and old brick and the ghost of whatever the machines had used for oil.

What she knows about the building's history: the textile machine hooks in the ceiling. The loading doors on the ground floor that still work. The east wall with the ghost of a sign that someone has been trying to read for years — she asked around and nobody agrees on what it says. She thinks the first word is "north." The second person she asked said "hour." The third said she was seeing things.

The neighbors she knows: the ones on the second floor who were here before her, who know things about the plumbing that she learned from them. The newer people on the fourth, who arrived a year ago with a lot of plants that now live in the high-ceilinged window light. The ground floor space that serves various purposes and whose double doors are open in spring.

In summer the high ceilings are an advantage. The heat goes up and stays up and the living space stays cool until eight or nine. She learned this in her first summer.

She wouldn't trade the windows.
