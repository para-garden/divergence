He's up at four. The apples are already boxed from the night before but there's always something — the crates need checking, the van needs loading in the right order so the fragile things don't end up under the heavy things. The Baldwins go in last.

He's been at this market for fourteen years. Before that his father was at this market. The stall has a location because of this history — not the best location, but known, and known matters. People find him because they found his father, or because someone told them where he was, or because they were looking for the apples that bruise easily and you can't find those at the big market because the big market can't move them fast enough.

He knows which field the apples came from if you want to know. Most people don't want to know. A few do, and he tells them in the specific way of someone who is pleased to be asked.

By six the stall is up. By eight he's seen all the regular faces and had several conversations he could summarize in one sentence each. By ten he knows roughly how the day will go. In October he's sold out of the Baldwins by noon.

He eats at the stall — something his wife made, or something from the bakery on the corner if he's in a hurry. He's usually not in a hurry. The market day has its own tempo. You learn it.

Home by two, because the van needs to go back, because there's always something to do at the farm, because two is when the day has earned its ending. He's been doing this long enough that the ending feels right.
