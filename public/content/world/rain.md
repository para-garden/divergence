The gutter by the corner bakery sounds like it's thinking out loud. Water running fast over a stuck ginkgo leaf, then loose, then the next one.

Ines pulls her bag over her head between the door and the overhang — not because it matters, she's already a little wet, but because it's the thing you do. She stops under the awning. Watches. A kid a few doors down has given up entirely and is just standing in it, looking up.

The smell is wet concrete and something sweet from the bakery exhaust and green from the planter boxes above the hardware shop, all at once, which you don't notice until it rains and it gets specific.

Bikes don't slow much. The tires sound different — that particular wet-road hiss. The riders have their hoods up or they don't, same as usual, like it's already accounted for.

From the second floor the street is just movement and reflections, the shop signs doubled in the water on the pavement, orange and white, wobbling when something passes.

Ines decides not to wait. Steps back into it. The bakery is only two blocks, and she's already wet.
