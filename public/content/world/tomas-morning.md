He's at the kiln space by seven, which is before anyone else arrives. He doesn't do this because it's efficient — he does it because the hour before people arrive is the hour that's his. The clay doesn't have an opinion about the time of day. The quiet does.

He checks the pieces from the last firing first. He does this the same way every time: lifts each one, turns it, looks at it in the light from the east window. Most of them are what they were going to be. Occasionally something surprises him — in either direction, occasionally better than he expected, occasionally the kiln did something unexpected and there's a failure he'll have to account for. He doesn't spend long on the failures. He understands why they failed and moves on.

His own work: right now a series he's been working on for a month, something about proportion, about the specific relationship between the foot and the lip that he's been trying to find and keeps finding slightly off. He's closer. He knows he's closer because the recent ones bother him less. You get somewhere by degrees.

He sweeps the studio before the first person arrives. This is also not efficiency. It's the way he marks the transition from his hour to the hour that's shared. The floor is clean. People can come in.

By eight there are sometimes people already at the door. He doesn't mind. He has what he needed from the morning. The rest of it is what it is — people making things, some better than others, all of them doing the thing, which is what matters.

He pours coffee from the thermos he brought. The east window light is different by now, higher, the early quality gone. The studio is just the studio.
