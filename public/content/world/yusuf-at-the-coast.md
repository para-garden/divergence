The tide was further out than he expected. The sand between the waterline and the dry part was darker, packed flat, and he walked on it because the packed part didn't give under his feet the way the dry part did — and he wanted to not sink.

The city had surfaces. Pavement, wood, old tile in the entryway. Here the surface kept changing: the packed sand, then a shelf of small round stones, then a low shelf of rock with water sitting in the hollows. He stopped at the rock and crouched. Something small moved in one of the pools. He waited until it stopped and then he could see it — a crab no bigger than his thumbnail, sitting still now that he was still.

He hadn't brought anything down with him. He'd just woken up before the others and come out.

The light was low and gray-orange, the kind of light that makes everything a little more itself. He could smell it — salt and something underneath the salt, something colder, something that didn't have a name. He breathed it in until it stopped being new and just became the air.

He stood back up. Fen had said something the night before about the rock shelf, about how far you could walk it at low tide before you had to turn back. He'd been half listening. He looked both ways down the coast — the shelf curved out of sight in either direction — and picked a direction and started walking.
