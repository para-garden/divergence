The radio had been Daphne's for nine years. She'd fixed it twice — once the power supply, once a corroded tuner contact that took three sessions to find. It sat on the kitchen shelf and had a sound she liked, a warmth that newer things don't have because newer things aren't designed for warmth.

The third time: she opened it and found the circuit board had developed cracks from heat cycling over the years, and the cracks had propagated in ways that weren't economically or practically reparable. Not everything. The tuner was fine. But the amplifier stage was not and the damage was the kind that spreads. She sat with it for twenty minutes, checking things she already knew she'd checked.

Some things are just done.

She put it in the materials box — the parts that are still good go somewhere useful, the speaker especially — and let herself be briefly annoyed, which is different from grief but is adjacent to it when something has been reliable for nine years. She'd bought it secondhand already old. It had been someone else's radio for a long time before.

She didn't immediately replace it. She went three weeks without one. She'd forgotten that silence was an option. Then she found something at the market — newer, not the same warmth, but good, and the person selling it had the manual.

The speaker from the old one went into something a kid at the workshop was building. The circuit board went into a jar of components. The case went to someone making something decorative with old cases.

Nothing of it is gone. It's just not a radio anymore.
