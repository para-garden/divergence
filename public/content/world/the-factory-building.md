The ceilings are high because they used to have to be. Six meters in the main hall — the kind of height that made sense when there were machines in here that needed it. The machines have been gone for eighty years. The height stayed.

The building was a textile factory, then something administrative that nobody quite agrees on, then empty for a decade, then apartments starting in the nineties and filling in slowly as the neighborhood came back around it. The conversion was piecemeal — not designed all at once, added to and altered as people moved in and changed things. The south end still has industrial windows, fifteen centimeters thick at the sill, because nobody got around to replacing them and then it turned out the light they let in was good.

The people who live here now live in high-ceilinged apartments with thick walls and slightly idiosyncratic plumbing. They know which pipes make noise in cold weather and why. The idiosyncratic plumbing has been fixed in places and tolerated in others. Toleration has its own logic.

There's a shared space on the ground floor that was originally the loading area — double doors facing the street, still present, still functional. People use it for various things: occasional market, kid bikes, someone's kiln until they found another space. The double doors open on spring days and the ground floor becomes semi-exterior.

You can see what the building was if you know to look. The hooks for overhead equipment are still in the ceiling. The wall on the east side has the ghost of a painted sign — weather-worn, most of the letters gone, the shape of letters remaining. Someone has been trying to figure out what it said for years.
