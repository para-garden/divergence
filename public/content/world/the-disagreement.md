They meant the same thing. That's what made it more annoying, not less — they were trying to say the same thing and the words kept landing differently.

Ines said the project was going wrong because the framing was off. Daphne said the framing was fine, the execution was off. Ines said the execution was a symptom of the framing. Daphne said that if you keep going up in abstraction you can make anything a framing problem. Ines said: well, can't you? This is when it became something.

It was about a mutual friend's thing — a project they'd both been asked to give feedback on, separately, and had given contradictory feedback, and were now comparing notes. Daphne thought hers had been correct and was surprised by Ines's. Ines thought the same in reverse.

The conversation got quieter as it went on, which with Ines means it was getting sharper, not softer. Daphne recognized this and let it happen. She doesn't retreat from a conversation going quiet. She says less.

By the end — maybe forty minutes, coffee getting cold — they had found the actual thing: they'd both been right about different parts of the problem and had been arguing as if the parts were the whole. This is not a satisfying resolution in the sense of someone winning. It's satisfying in the sense of the thing becoming clear.

They talked for another twenty minutes about something else and left on normal terms. This is how they are. Direct, willing to be wrong, not precious about being right. The friendship survives its own tension because neither of them expects it not to.

Ines texted the mutual friend with a slightly revised note. Daphne did too. Their notes were still not identical.
