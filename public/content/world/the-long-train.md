Four hours, city to city. You pack light because you know how to pack light by now. You get a window seat or you don't. If you don't, the seat you get is still fine.

The first hour is still your city — suburbs giving way, familiar names on platforms, then unfamiliar ones, then nothing but the backs of things. Industrial buildings with their loading doors open. Gardens with actual gardens in them. The occasional field you wouldn't know how to name the crop of.

Second hour is countryside. It's not dramatically different from the first hour in any one moment. You just look up and you're clearly somewhere that isn't a city. Hills in the middle distance that you don't have names for. A river that might be the same river for a long time. Someone is fishing from a bank. Nobody is fishing from a bank. That's the kind of thing you almost see.

You eat whatever you brought. You read or you don't. The person in the seat across is asleep by the second hour, which seems like the right response to a long train.

Something happens around hour three where the light changes or the landscape changes or you just feel it differently — you're not going somewhere anymore, you're arriving. The city you're headed to starts to present itself: the backs of its industrial things, its gardens, its river if it has one. You look at it the way you looked at your own city once when you arrived.

At the platform someone is waiting or no one is. You know where you're going or you figure it out. The body adjusts to being still.

You spend a few days. You take the train back. By the third hour you know you're almost home.
