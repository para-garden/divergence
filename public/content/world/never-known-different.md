She's twenty-two. She grew up here, in this city, on this floor. Her parents aren't worried about retirement in the way that means they don't sleep. When her sister got sick she got better and the getting-better cost nothing that mattered. When she decided not to go to university she didn't go to university; when she decided to go later she went later. These were just decisions.

She doesn't think about any of this. It's just her life.

She went through a period at seventeen of reading about other ways things could be and felt the familiar teenage feeling of wanting to have been tested — to have struggled against something, to have built something from nothing. Now she's twenty-two and less interested in struggle as a proof of self. She's interested in what she's building. The building is the thing.

Her parents worried about her in the ways that parents worry: whether she had friends, whether she was happy, what direction she was moving. Not survival — direction. What kind of life do you want? That's the question they asked, not as a luxury but as a genuine inquiry.

She doesn't know people who are worried about things she isn't worried about. Her people have their problems — the ones humans have always had, the ones that don't go away because the floor is there. Connection, meaning, direction. How to be a person.

When she leaves the city for a while and reads about how it was in other places and other times, she feels a thing she can't name. Not guilt, exactly. Something more like: *oh*. And then she comes home and it's just home and she gets on with it.

She's twenty-two. She has things to do.
