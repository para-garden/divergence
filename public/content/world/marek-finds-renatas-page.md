Marek had been staring at the grain for twenty minutes when he gave up and went looking.

The problem was the filler. He'd used the wrong kind — he knew that now, could see it in the way the light caught the surface wrong, a flatness where there should have been depth. He'd searched around, found some forum posts from fifteen years ago, a video from someone who clearly didn't know what they were doing. Then he found her page.

It took him a minute to figure out what he was looking at. The layout was hers — not a template, or not just a template — there was a sidebar with a sketch of a hand plane that someone had drawn, not a logo, and the background was a color he'd describe as the inside of a cardboard box. Good color. The navigation was in a font she'd chosen for reasons she hadn't explained and didn't need to. The page loaded in pieces, slowly, in a way that felt like arriving somewhere.

She'd written a long entry about stripping technique. Not an article — a working document, notes she'd added to over years, with a line near the top that said *updated because I was wrong about the shellac part, see below.* Below was a correction. The correction was as long as the original. She'd been wrong, she'd figured out why, she'd written it down.

The filler section was near the bottom. She'd made his exact mistake. She'd described it in a way that made him feel less stupid — not because she was kind about it, but because she was specific about it, and the specificity meant she'd actually been there. *The problem is you're sealing over it instead of filling into it.* Yes. That was exactly it. She had a fix.

He read the whole page. Then he read the repair logs, which were in a separate section with dates going back to 2019. A side table she'd found in someone's trash. A wardrobe that had been painted seventeen times. A chair with a cracked leg that she'd documented with photographs and notes about what the crack told her about how the chair had been used. The photographs were just photographs, not styled, shot on a phone or maybe a camera from ten years ago. The light in them was the light of her workshop.

He went back to the cabinet. He fixed the filler.

The next day there was a note on his page.

He almost missed it — he didn't check his page that often, it was mostly a place to put things for himself, repair logs and reference notes, a few photographs of work in progress. But the notification was there, small, the way he'd set it up. Someone had left a note.

*Renata Vásquez, from Brno. I saw your cabinet. You had the same filler problem I had twenty years ago — looks like you figured it out but if you want to talk through the grain raising step I can save you some time. Good luck with it.*

That was all. A contact form link at the bottom.

He sat with it for a minute. She'd been on his page. She'd read whatever he'd written about the cabinet — not much, a few lines, a photograph — and recognized something in it, and found it worth saying something about. Not to be helpful in the abstract. Because she'd been there and had something that might be useful.

He wrote back.

The grain raising step had in fact been his next problem. They wrote back and forth for a week, short messages, practical. She sent a photograph of a joint she was proud of. He sent one back. At some point he asked how long she'd been doing this and she said long enough to have made most of the mistakes at least once and some of them twice.

Her page was still open in a tab. He kept going back to it. Not for any specific thing. Just because it was there — a place someone had made, with years of her attention in it, findable by anyone who went looking.
