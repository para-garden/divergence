The pool has been here since before anyone currently using it was born. The tiles are the original tiles — white with a blue stripe at the waterline — and they have that specific smoothness of old ceramic that new tiles don't have. The smell is the smell. You stop noticing it two minutes after you get in and notice it again when you leave.

It's open at six. The people who come at six are the serious ones — the ones who swim the same number of laps in the same amount of time three times a week because it's the thing they do and they do it. Nobody talks much at six. The lane dividers have a specific motion in the wake. The light comes in at a low angle from the high windows.

Later: children's lessons in the shallow end, various arrangements of people in lanes, the occasional chaos of a group that didn't realize how many lanes were in use. The lifeguard sits on the stand and watches in the way lifeguards watch — aware without appearing to be.

The water is cold. Everyone says it's cold every time as though it will at some point be warm. It won't. It has never been warm. The cold is part of the experience of the pool and the people who swim there have accepted this without naming their acceptance.

Renata swims on Wednesdays. She has swum on Wednesdays for eleven years with a break for her hip and then resuming when her hip resolved, which took eight months. She goes to the far lane, swims her laps, knows everyone in the far lane by sight, knows a few by name. The cold doesn't bother her anymore or it does and she's decided not to notice.

The pool doesn't need to justify itself. It's just here. People swim.
