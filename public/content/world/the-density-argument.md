The argument is about how dense the city should get. Not whether it should get denser — everyone agrees the city is growing, people are arriving, that's not the argument. The argument is about what kind of density.

One position: more height, more concentration, in specific corridors. Keep the farm at the edge. Keep the field fifteen minutes out. Density has its own logic — walkable, vertical, the bakery on the ground floor because enough people pass it. Concentrating people concentrates what serves them.

The other position: the fields are already closer than they look. The city is already dense enough in most places. The risk of concentrating further is what you're concentrating — not just people, but the specific kind of city that forms around density, which has certain qualities and lacks others. A five-story city has courtyards. A twenty-story city has a different logic.

Both sides can point to cities they're right about. Both have failures too, cities where the approach didn't produce what it promised.

Renata has been in the second-position camp for thirty years. Her position has evolved — she used to be more absolute about it. The young planner on the third floor of the factory building is in the first-position camp and they've talked about it, not combatively, in the way you talk about things with someone who has thirty years more context than you. She's learned things from him. He's learned things from her. Neither has moved.

This is what they argue about here: how much, where, at what cost to what. Real competing goods. No obvious answer. The city keeps being built one decision at a time and both of them have opinions about each decision and neither of them controls any of it.

That's fine. The decisions get made in forums and meetings and votes and eventually in what gets built, which is either what someone decided or what nobody stopped. This is how cities are.
