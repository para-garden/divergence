It doesn't announce itself. One day the coats are necessary, and then a week later they're optional, and then a week after that you've left yours at home and you're fine. The transition happens while you're doing other things.

The market changes. The winter roots thin out and something green comes in — radishes, then asparagus, then things that need warmth and take longer. The vendors who were wearing two layers are in one layer. The smell of the market is different: lighter, wetter, more alive in a way that's hard to describe to someone who hasn't been standing at a market stall in March for several years.

The linden leafs out. This is an event that people have opinions about — when exactly, how fast, whether this year is early or late. The linden doesn't care about the opinions but the opinions arrive every year anyway, which suggests they're more about the people having them than the tree.

Kids reappear in the courtyard with the same quality as the first warm Saturday every year — like they've been in storage since October. The bikes come out. Someone opens a window that's been closed since November and forgets to close it.

The light changes last. Not last chronologically — the light starts to feel different in February — but last to register. One evening you're walking home and you notice you can still see and it's six-thirty. You think: oh. You don't think anything else about it. You keep walking.

There's a specific week in April where everything is happening at once and the city has a particular energy that doesn't last, just that week, this combination of warm enough and still new. People sit outside who weren't sitting outside yesterday. The bakery runs out of bread before noon on Saturday.

Spring recalibrates everything slightly. The city adjusts. People adjust. They go on.
