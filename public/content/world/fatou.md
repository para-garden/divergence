After twelve years the city is in her body. She knows how to walk in it. Which side of the street catches the wind, which tram stop has a specific delay at rush hour, the particular weight of the market bag at the end of a full shop. She doesn't think about any of this. It's just where she lives.

She came here for work — something specific, a specific project, a person she was going to work with. The project ended. The person is still a friend, they see each other when they see each other. She stayed because by then she'd built things, which is the reason that actually holds.

Her family is in Dakar. Her mother and her two sisters and the neighborhood she knows from the inside — the specific morning smell of it, the way the heat sits differently there, the conversations that happen standing on the street that take forty minutes because everyone has something to add and that's how it's supposed to go. She goes back every two years or when something pulls her back sooner.

The food cooperative here is not the neighborhood network at home. She knows this. It's its own thing — more formal in some ways, less in others, organized around the tool that works reliably, conducted through messages and a shared schedule rather than the standing conversations on the street. She does the Thursday slot. She's met most of the people who do other slots, some well, some just well enough to swap with.

She has learned to tell when a vendor's produce is actually fresh versus has been sitting. This is her mother's skill, learned by watching the market at home, applied to a different market in a different city. The knowledge transferred. Some things do.
