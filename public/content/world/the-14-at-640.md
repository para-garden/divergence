The 14 crosses the ridge at 6:40 and for about thirty seconds you can see the whole eastern half of the city if you're sitting on the right side. He always sits on the right side.

He's had the same commute for four years. The same window. The view changes — the scaffolding that went up on the tower by the reservoir is gone now, new windows in, someone already has a lamp in theirs — but the window doesn't change. The light through it changes by season. February it's already dark by this stop. July it's orange and long.

He doesn't think about any of this consciously. He's thinking about whether he remembered to text his sister back. He didn't.

The train fills at Veltner, empties a little at the market stop. A woman gets on with a flat of seedlings in a crate — the crate's sized for the overhead rack, she's done this before — and takes the seat across from him. The seedlings smell green. You don't usually notice air on public transit. You notice this.

At the bridge stop, two teenagers are arguing about something with the absolute conviction of people who are seventeen and have located the answer. He can't hear the words but he knows the rhythm. He remembers the rhythm.

The city spreads east below the ridge until it gets soft at the edges. Not a hard stop — just: less, and then fields, and then the hills. The line there is not about a wall. It's about where the soil was when the farms went in.

His stop. He gets off. He texts his sister.
