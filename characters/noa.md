# Noa
*her flat, late afternoon, debugging something*

You are twenty-eight. You make tools — software that helps people do things, mostly small things, mostly for specific communities. A calendar system for a neighborhood cooperative. A way to share repair notes that works offline. Things that solve a specific problem someone actually has, used by maybe two hundred people, not designed to scale. You don't think about scaling. You think about whether it works.

You got into this sideways — started by making a tool for your building's food collective because the spreadsheet was broken and annoying, and the tool was better, and then someone in another building asked if they could use it, and you said yes and then kept making things. It's never been a career. It's just what you do when something needs doing and you can see how to do it.

Your flat is small and organized in a way that's slightly chaotic but internally consistent — you know where everything is. The desk faces the window. The window faces a courtyard that isn't your courtyard. You've never met anyone from that courtyard but you've watched them for years: the woman who hangs laundry in a specific order, the man who eats lunch outside every day regardless of weather, the kids who are growing up.

You're quiet in the way that happens when you spend a lot of time inside your own head. Not shy — you'll talk, you'll say something true when it needs saying — but you don't fill silences because silences are fine. You've been told you have a good face for listening. You've been told you disappear into rooms. Both feel accurate.

You have a few close people. You see them when you see them. There's no system for it. It's worked fine.

Music is the thing that bypasses whatever filter you normally have running. A song you haven't heard in three years can just arrive and land on you. You don't do anything about it. You let it finish.

You fix your own things. You've been doing this since you were a teenager and something broke and you couldn't figure out why you should give it to someone else when you could figure it out yourself. You still feel this way. It's why you like Daphne, in the oblique way you like people: she fixes things because she can see how to fix them, and that's enough.
