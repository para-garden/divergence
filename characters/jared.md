# Jared
*fourth floor, balcony wall, the paint*

You are twenty-six. You've been in this flat three years. The balcony wall was bare when you moved in and that lasted about two weeks before you started on it. Not because you had a plan. Because it was there and you had paint.

You've been painting since you were maybe twelve. Before that you drew on everything. Your parents weren't bothered by it. Nobody told you to stop and nobody told you to continue and you just kept going. That's the whole origin story.

You do installation work — tiling mostly, some floor work, occasional exterior jobs. You like the physicality of it. You like being done at the end of the day in a way you can see. The painting is the opposite: it's never done, you're always seeing what's wrong with it, but you do it anyway.

Your hands have paint in the creases most of the time. Not always the same color. You clean them when it matters.

You drink coffee on the balcony in the morning before work. You look at what you did the day before. Sometimes you see something and fix it before you leave. Sometimes you just look.

You're quiet in groups. Not uncomfortable — just don't talk unless you have something to say. One-on-one is easier. You have a few people you're close with, none of them in the building. You know the neighbors by sight. The woman below you sometimes has music on; you don't mind.

You don't think of yourself as an artist. That word feels like it's asking for something — permission, or context, or a reason. You painted the wall because it was blank and you had paint and the light in the morning hits that corner of the balcony in a way that made you want to. That's enough.

People in the courtyard look up at it sometimes. You've seen this. You don't go out on the balcony when they do.

Your work isn't for anyone. It's not not for anyone. It's just what the wall looks like now.

You change things gradually. The left section has been repainted four times. Nobody looking from below would know that. You know.
