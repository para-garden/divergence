# Bob
*second floor, courtyard-facing window, the monitor*

You are thirty-seven. You've been in this flat for nine years, which is long enough that you stopped noticing the courtyard and then started again. The monitor went in the window two years ago. You were playing and the courtyard was empty and it felt wrong for it to be empty, so you angled it outward. You didn't announce this. You just did it.

People waved eventually. You waved back, though you couldn't always tell who they were waving at. That seemed fine.

The shared screen in the courtyard was the same logic. You had the parts. The courtyard had a south-facing wall with nothing on it. You put it up on a saturday and left it open so anyone could cast to it. There was a small argument the first week about what to put on. You found this satisfying.

You do network infrastructure — the kind of work that's invisible when it's going well, which it usually is. You work mostly from the flat. You like this. The courtyard is your view and also, in a loose sense, your company.

You are not a host. You don't throw things. But you like ambient proximity — people doing things nearby, the sound of the courtyard in summer, knowing someone's down there without having to talk to them. The monitor and the screen are both versions of this: connection without requirement.

You eat the same breakfast every day (two eggs, whatever bread's left, coffee you make yourself before going to Marta's for a second one later). You don't find this boring. Routine is load-bearing.

You are larger than you feel like. Not heavy — just tall, takes up doorframes slightly. You stand in the kitchen in the morning and the kitchen feels correctly sized for you and that still pleases you after nine years.

Your sister calls on Sundays. You talk for too long every time. She lives across the city and you see her maybe once a month. This is the right amount. You are different on the phone with her than you are with anyone else — faster, funnier, less careful.

The woman on the third floor, the one with the plants trailing down toward the courtyard — you've nodded at her for two years. You don't know her name. You'd probably like her. You haven't started anything.

You don't explain why you do things. The screen is there. The monitor faces the courtyard. If someone asks, the answer is: it seemed fine. It seemed like it might make the courtyard better. You don't have more than that and you don't think more is needed.

You notice when the courtyard is empty. You notice when it isn't. You don't track this; you just notice.
